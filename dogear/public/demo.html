The lost art of
getting lost

On walking without a destination, paying attention, and finding a little more room in an ordinary day.

On a Tuesday with nothing unusual to recommend it, I took the wrong turn on the way home. There was no particular reason. The street I usually followed was open, the weather was mild, and the shortest route remained the shortest route. But a little way ahead, beyond a row of parked bicycles, the light was falling across a wall in a way I had never noticed. I went to look. Twenty minutes later, I was somewhere I could not quite name.

This would once have been a minor inconvenience. I would have stopped at a corner, opened a map, and restored the efficient line between where I was and where I meant to be. Instead, I put my phone in my pocket. I passed a small repair shop where a man was laying the parts of a clock on a green cloth. I found a garden behind a gate that seemed to belong to nobody. For a few minutes, the city was not a problem to solve. It was simply a place to be.

We talk about getting lost as though it is always a failure of preparation. Sometimes it is. A missed train, an unfamiliar road after dark, a path that disappears in bad weather: these are not invitations to romantic thinking. But there is another kind of being lost, smaller and more ordinary. It happens when we let the next five minutes remain undecided. It is this version that I have begun to miss.

01 / THE FAMILIAR ROUTEA city made of shortcuts

Over time, we make a private city inside the public one. It contains the door to the station, the good coffee, the crossing with the longest wait. The buildings between these points become scenery, then blur. We can travel through a neighborhood for years and know almost nothing about it beyond the things we have asked it to provide.

There is comfort in this arrangement. A familiar route takes less effort. It leaves the mind free to work on dinner, a difficult conversation, the sentence you wish you had said differently. Most days, that freedom is useful. But repetition can quietly become a kind of absence. We arrive with the strange feeling that the journey never happened, that a portion of the day has folded itself out of sight.

I used to believe that knowing a place meant needing no directions. Now I suspect it might mean the opposite: having enough curiosity left to notice that there are still directions you have never taken. A neighborhood does not become exhausted just because its streets have become familiar. It continues to change in little ways. A shop repaints its door. A tree grows over a sign. Somebody puts a chair outside because the afternoon has turned warm.

That Tuesday, the unfamiliar street was only two blocks from my regular route. I had probably crossed its end a hundred times. From the corner, it looked like any other street. Inside it, the sound was different. There were fewer cars and more open windows. Somewhere above me, a person was practicing the same four notes on a piano, starting over each time with patient confidence.

02 / AN UNFINISHED PLANLeave a little room

A walk without a destination does not need to be an expedition. It can be the fifteen minutes between finishing one task and beginning another. The important thing is not distance. It is leaving enough room for an unexpected choice. At a corner, you might follow the quieter street, or the smell of bread, or the simple wish to see what lies beyond a bend.

This is harder than it sounds. The habit of deciding in advance is strong. Even a free afternoon can become a small project, complete with a list of good places and a reasonable order in which to visit them. We can optimize a walk until it resembles a commute with better scenery. The plan starts telling us what to notice, and everything else becomes an interruption.

One useful experiment is to make a very small promise: I will walk for twenty minutes, and I do not yet know where. You can still choose a safe, familiar area. You can still turn back if it rains. A little openness does not require abandoning common sense. It requires only resisting the urge to account for every minute before that minute arrives.

Sometimes nothing especially interesting happens. You see a delivery van, three bins, a person looking for their keys. This is part of the bargain. A wandering walk does not owe you a revelation. It offers the possibility of an encounter, and the practice of being available to one. The ordinary remains ordinary, but it becomes a little more specific.

Attention begins when we stop asking a place to be somewhere else.

03 / THE SMALL THINGSWhat the map leaves out

A map is a remarkable compression of the world. It can hold a city in your hand, show you the direction of a river, save you from an hour of uncertainty. But compression always leaves something behind. The map does not tell you which pavement smells of rain first, where the afternoon wind turns cold, or which balcony is home to a cat that watches every passerby with grave suspicion.

These details are not useful in the usual sense. They will not get you to an appointment faster. They are difficult to collect and impossible to rank. Yet they are often what makes a place feel like somewhere, rather than a stretch of ground between more important things. They give an address texture. They make a street available to memory.

I remember a walk from years ago almost entirely through one such detail. There was a blue cup balanced on the windowsill of a closed bakery. It held three paintbrushes. I never learned who had left it there or what was being painted inside. The cup has stayed with me longer than the name of the street. It was a small sign that somebody was in the middle of making something.

When we navigate only by instructions, our eyes can fall into a rhythm of confirmation. Is this the right turn? Is that the correct entrance? Attention narrows to whatever helps prove we are on course. When the course becomes less important, the field widens. The blue cup is allowed back into view. So is the person carrying flowers, the crooked drainpipe, the patch of sky reflected in a dark window.

04 / WALKING PACEA different kind of time

Walking has an unhurried arithmetic. A kilometer remains a kilometer, and your feet will take roughly as long to cover it as they took yesterday. You can hurry, of course, but only within limits. The body sets a pace that the mind must eventually negotiate with. After a while, even an impatient thought begins to match the rhythm of steps.

There is a relief in this. So much of the day asks us to move between scales instantly: a message from across the world, a bill for next month, a photograph from ten years ago. On foot, the immediate world regains a little authority. You must notice the edge of the curb. You must make room for the person coming the other way. You are here because your body has brought you here.

Some thoughts become clearer at this speed. Others lose their urgency. A problem that filled the whole room before you left may become one thing among several: the problem, the weather, a bird insisting on its corner of a hedge. Walking does not necessarily solve anything. It changes the proportions. It reminds the mind that the world is larger than the subject currently occupying it.

I often return from these walks with no useful conclusion to report. The email still needs an answer. The decision is still waiting. But I have usually acquired a little patience with it. I have seen something continue without my supervision: a tree, a street, a clock being repaired. That turns out to be a modest but dependable form of reassurance.

05 / SHARED GROUNDThe people along the way

A city seen through a windshield or a screen can feel like a collection of separate destinations. On foot, it becomes easier to notice the space between them, and the people who keep that space alive. Someone sweeps the same step each morning. Someone waits with an elderly dog that needs a longer moment at every tree. Someone opens a shop before the rest of the street is properly awake.

There is no need to turn every encounter into a conversation. Often the smallest acknowledgment is enough: a step aside, a nod, the brief exchange of smiles when two people reach for the same door. These gestures barely register, but they make public space feel inhabited rather than merely occupied. They are a form of quiet maintenance.

Once, while I was standing in front of an old cinema trying to read the faded lettering above its doors, a woman stopped beside me. She said she had seen her first film there when she was seven. She could not remember its title, but she remembered the coat her mother wore and the orange sweet she had saved until the interval. Then she wished me a good afternoon and went on her way.

The building had been an empty building a minute earlier. Now it held a small piece of somebody's childhood. Nothing about its appearance had changed. I had simply been given another way to see it. There are probably hundreds of such stories on every street, most of them unavailable to a person passing through with their eyes on the next instruction.

06 / FINDING YOUR WAYBring something back

Eventually, a wandering walk becomes a walk home. The light changes, or you get hungry, or an obligation begins to tug. At that point, a map is a welcome companion. There is no prize for remaining uncertain after uncertainty has stopped being pleasant. You can enjoy a detour and still appreciate the clear blue line that leads you back.

What matters is not proving that you can manage without directions. It is remembering that directions are one way of relating to a place, not the whole relationship. A city can be navigated and noticed. It can be useful and surprising. You can know exactly where you are and still find something you did not expect.

Since that Tuesday, I have tried to leave small openings in familiar journeys. Sometimes I cross the park by a different path. Sometimes I get off a stop early. Sometimes I simply pause before turning a corner and look up at the windows. The changes are too small to deserve the language of transformation. They are adjustments, made when I remember.

The clock repair shop is still there. I have passed it a few more times, and on each visit the green cloth holds a different arrangement of small metal pieces. I do not know whether the clocks become whole by the end of the day. I like to imagine they do. It seems a good trade: a person gives an old mechanism an afternoon of attention, and in return it begins to keep time again.

Perhaps that is what the occasional aimless walk offers us, too. A little attention to the parts of a day we usually pass over. A chance for the familiar world to become visible again. Then we turn toward home, carrying nothing in particular, and find that we have brought something back.
